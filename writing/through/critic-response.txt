1. **DESCRIPTION**

A council turns a route through Mara’s house into a public shortcut to the station. Commuters are largely courteous, but their traffic progressively dictates how she sits, eats and moves around her kitchen. She buys a trolley to help carry pans. Returning from buying milk, she joins the flow through her own house, watches someone move her trolley out of the way, and follows everyone out the back gate. The council’s counter records her passage.

2. **FEELING**

The opening is funny and alarming at once: an extraordinary invasion arrives as an accomplished administrative fact. The discomfort builds through small accommodations rather than confrontation. The commuters’ consideration briefly softens it, then makes it worse: everyone can behave pleasantly while Mara loses the use of her home. The ending produces a quiet shock—she has become another person passing through the place where she lives.

3. **READING**

The story concerns dispossession through reasonable-sounding adjustments, and the difference between measuring public benefit and recognizing private loss. Six minutes saved can be counted; Mara’s diminishing freedom in her kitchen cannot.

Her adaptation becomes evidence that the arrangement works. The inspector photographs the adjusted chair “without her in it”; the petition collector identifies her as “The kitchen”; the counter finally counts her as traffic. Each reduces her presence to something useful to the scheme.

The commuter’s ability to take his daughter to school matters. The benefit is real, which makes the conflict sharper than a simple portrait of selfish strangers. Likewise, Mara’s overlooked consultation envelope complicates her position without making the resulting intrusion proportionate.

4. **CRAFT**

The prose holds attention through precise domestic consequences. Toast placed above passing bags, the cello lifted over the fruit bowl, and Mara waiting with a hot pan against her stomach make the premise physically credible. The thumbprint in the paint gives the council’s abstract authority a small, careless human trace.

The strongest exchange is the inspector offering to hold the tape after Mara says, “That’s where I sit.” He understands her objection only as a practical problem he can help solve. The stranger answering her telephone similarly pushes consideration into appropriation without explaining the distinction.

The trolley earns its extended introduction because it represents Mara’s effort to preserve some agency, then becomes an obstruction others remove. The weekday progression supplies momentum, although it also makes the escalation feel conspicuously arranged.

The final procession is the largest strain: Mara follows the crowd completely out of her own house because the ending needs that image. Her earlier compliance prepares it, but the action remains more persuasive symbolically than psychologically. “It had counted her” is effective, though it underlines a meaning already clear from her position outside the gate.

5. **VERDICT**

**YES.** This is compelling short fiction: an absurd premise develops through convincing physical details and socially plausible exchanges. Its restraint lets courtesy and genuine public benefit coexist with profound intrusion. The ending is slightly engineered, but the accumulated loss of ordinary domestic freedom gives it force.
