1. **DESCRIPTION**

At a craft sale, Ruth points out that fellow potter Eva’s bowl is uneven while a customer is considering buying it. Eva loses the immediate sale, then sells the bowl at a discount when the customer returns. Their exchanges reveal resentment, especially on Ruth’s side: she once taught Eva, whose work now sells better. Afterwards, Ruth offers Eva and her son a lift, accepts an inconvenient detour, and makes room for them in her car.

2. **FEELING**

The story produces a steadily tightening social discomfort. Ruth’s warning is defensible, but its timing becomes increasingly suspect as her envy emerges. The customer’s “companionable smile” is especially painful: it makes Ruth an apparent ally in securing the discount.

The ending softens that discomfort without resolving it. Ruth’s cramped driving position makes her accommodation tangible. It feels like a small kindness performed by someone whose resentment has survived intact.

3. **READING**

The story explores how professional integrity, envy, and generosity can occupy the same person. Ruth genuinely notices a defect. She also remembers teaching Eva, measures their unequal sales, and invokes collective reputation—“It would have reflected on all of us”—when her personal justification is challenged.

Her self-awareness provides no easy escape. “There was no reason to keep remembering this” shows that she recognizes the pettiness of rehearsing her former authority, yet keeps doing it. Her offer of a lift follows the same pattern: she wants to help, wants Eva to spare her the inconvenience, and resents being taken at her word.

The title names imperfect pottery and suggests Ruth’s discomfort at being surpassed. The story also keeps testing whether flawed acts of care retain their value.

4. **CRAFT**

The prose holds attention through precise actions that carry social consequences. Eva stopping with her hand inside a box, straightening the cloth, and later concealing the glaze with her hand all make the bowl’s changing status visible. Ruth retrieving wrapping paper “that had not fallen” is an excellent physical expression of shame.

The cat dish earns its place because Ruth wraps it carefully despite the buyer’s intended use: her respect for her work extends beyond vanity. The boy’s rolls, raffle disappointment, and heater adjustments give him an independent presence while making Eva’s burdens concrete. The final seat adjustment is restrained and effective; the story ends on an action Ruth chooses not to reverse.

A few passages explain what the scenes already establish. The magazine photograph opposite Ruth’s price sheet is a somewhat schematic contrast between success and obscurity. The sentence explaining that Ruth hoped Eva would decline the lift, then disliked her for waiting, states the emotional mechanism unusually fully. Martin’s repeatedly moved landscape is observant and amusing, but briefly disperses the central tension.

These are modest reservations. The dialogue is particularly strong: “I just did,” “Before her,” and “I know how” expose the conflict without turning it into a speech.

5. **VERDICT**

**YES.** This is compelling short fiction through close observation and sustained moral ambiguity. Its small incident changes how every subsequent courtesy reads. Ruth remains capable of both spite and care, and the ending gives that contradiction a convincing physical form without claiming she has overcome it.
