## Verbatim response

1. **DESCRIPTION**

Irene recruits her brother Rob to pose as a competing buyer for her £180 sofa. He bungles the performance, then tries to buy it himself for £90. The real buyer, Denise, recognizes the arrangement and negotiates £160 plus £20 for Rob to help deliver it. Irene receives her money; Rob and Denise carry the sofa to the van, with the lift’s dimensions still to check.

2. **FEELING**

The opening creates amused anticipation: Rob’s question about sagging immediately suggests that Irene cannot count on him to follow instructions. That becomes social embarrassment when he sits down, exposes the faulty spring, and starts bargaining for himself. Denise’s composure keeps the discomfort light. By the end, I felt a modest warmth toward these people, whose practical cooperation survives their attempts to outmaneuver one another.

3. **READING**

The story concerns the gap between controlling a transaction and dealing with actual people. Irene scripts urgency; Denise simply waits. Rob is supposed to establish the sofa’s value but introduces his own needs and eventually finds a paid role carrying it.

The lemons make that gap tangible: arranged for the sales photograph, returned to their bag, then displayed again with the soft one hidden underneath. Irene continues managing appearances even after her larger deception fails. Yet her care with the sofa’s screws complicates any simple judgment of her. She wants the sale, but she also makes sure the small necessary pieces survive it.

4. **CRAFT**

The dialogue carries most of the pleasure. “Because you’re in a hurry” establishes the scheme economically, while “That’s between me and her” lets Denise puncture it without making a scene. Physical actions keep the conversation active: Rob removes his coat instead of leaving; Denise kneels on her blanket to inspect underneath; the tape measure clicks back during negotiation.

The coat caught in the kitchen door is especially good: the attempted private conference remains visibly clumsy. The screws and damaged bag earn their place by giving the ending a practical texture beyond the joke.

A few lines explain what is already legible. “Irene had expected the other buyer to make her act more quickly” spells out the setup’s reversal. The comparison between cleaning the recliner and negotiating away its stains feels more conspicuously clever than the surrounding prose. Rob’s claim that he mistook the photograph for another sofa also approaches sitcom convenience, though it works as an embarrassed excuse.

The replacement arriving tomorrow supplies convincing pressure, but arrives exactly when the negotiation needs it. The final lift question is a tidy callback to measurement; satisfying, though somewhat foreseeable.

5. **VERDICT**

**YES.** This is compelling small-scale comic fiction. Three distinct people emerge through bargaining, interruptions, and handling furniture. Its reversals are slightly neat, but the material details and understated cooperation give it substance beyond a failed sales trick.
